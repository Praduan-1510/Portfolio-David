What is broken today

Eight failures, all of them ordinary, none of them anybody's fault. Every one of them is a place where the patient is quietly asked to do the system's job. Under each is what CareBridge does instead — and where in this prototype you can watch it happen.

01

The patient is the integration layer

They carry a paper prescription to a pharmacy, a paper requisition to a lab, and a printed report back to a doctor. Every handoff loses something.

One record. A prescription written in the consult is fillable in two taps, an ordered test becomes a booking, and the result returns to the doctor who asked for it.

02

Discovery is guesswork

Someone with abdominal pain does not know whether they need Gastroenterology, General Medicine or Emergency. An alphabetical list of departments does not help them.

Describe it in your own words, or point at a body map. A red-flag check runs underneath every lane and sends an urgent presentation to Emergency before anything else.

03

Prescriptions are opaque

Patients cannot read them, do not know what the drug is for, cannot tell whether a cheaper version exists, and cannot find a store that has it in stock.

Structured prescriptions written out in full, plain-language monographs, generic substitutes with the saving shown, and store stock with the time it was last updated.

04

Reports vanish

Results arrive as a text-message link that expires, an email attachment that gets lost, or a paper copy you collect in person during working hours.

A permanent vault — searchable, trended over time, downloadable to your own device, and shareable on a link that stops working when you say it should.

05

Families are invisible to the system

One person manages care for a child, a spouse and an ageing parent. Most apps still assume one account is one patient.

Five profiles under one login, with graduated consent, proxy booking and separate records. Whose care you are looking at is named in the top bar at all times.

06

An emergency gets the dermatology interface

The same three-step booking flow serves a mole check and a suspected heart attack, and the person having the heart attack is the one least able to work it out.

A dedicated emergency layer — triage, ambulance, emergency-room finder, urgent advice — behind an affordance that is two taps from anywhere and that nothing else can cover.

07

Doctors are drowning in documentation

The notes get finished at nine in the evening, from memory, which is exactly how detail is lost and how a clinic day becomes a fourteen-hour one.

Order entry, templated notes, and a clinician-signed AI draft where every claim is linked back to the line in the chart it came from. Nothing is filed unsigned.

08

Money is a surprise

The price arrives after the service. The insurance answer arrives after the price. Neither is available at the moment the decision is actually made.

An estimate before booking with insurance already applied, eligibility checked up front, and an itemised bill afterwards that matches what was quoted.

One episode of care

Margaret Reyes is seventy-two, has type 2 diabetes and hypertension, and lives with her daughter-in-law Amanda, who manages the household's health. This is one episode, nine steps, left to right. Every step opens the screen that implements it.

Scroll the strip, or use the arrows.

This line is the whole thesis. A teleconsult app stops at step four. An e-pharmacy starts at step six with no idea why the medicine was prescribed. A diagnostics aggregator owns steps seven and eight and hands back a PDF. Every player owns a segment; nobody owns the episode. CareBridge is specified so that step nine feeds step one — which is why the north-star metric is resolved care journeys, not downloads or monthly actives.

Thirty-two departments, seven journeys

Hand-building a flow per specialty is how health apps die: the sixth department takes as long as the first and the thirtieth never ships. CareBridge defines seven journey archetypes instead. A department declares which ones it uses and supplies configuration — intake questions, services, scheduling rules, required documents, follow-up policy. Adding a specialty is a data change, not a release.

Showing all 32 departments.

Each department carries one of fourteen categorical tints, separated in hue and in lightness so any two stay distinguishable under deuteranopia, under protanopia and in grayscale print. The tint is identity and wayfinding only — it never carries clinical status, and it never appears without the department's name beside it.

Every value knows where it came from

A blood pressure taken in a clinic and one taken on a kitchen table are not the same measurement, and a chart that draws them identically is telling a small lie every time it renders. This is the most distinctive thing in the design system and the hardest one to give up.

Provenance is shape, not colour

Clinic measured

Taken by a clinician or a facility device. A filled disc with a keyline. This is the only grading that can trigger a critical-value escalation on its own.

Home device

A connected, calibrated monitor — Margaret's Omron HEM-7156T. A hollow ring. Trusted for trend, named in the caption, never mistaken for a clinic reading.

Self reported

A number the patient typed in. A dashed ring. It belongs in the record — it is often the only reading anyone has — but the chart says plainly where it came from.

Estimated

A model filling a gap. A dashed diamond, and the whole span it influences is dashed with it. An estimate never contributes to an average, a threshold or an alert.

Shape survives what colour does not: a grayscale print-out clipped into a paper chart, a red-green colour vision deficiency, a phone in direct sunlight, a projector with the contrast wound down. Colour only ever supports what the shape has already said.

Two more rules fall out of it. A gap in the data is drawn as a gap — the line breaks rather than quietly inventing a reading that never happened. And the interpolation is monotone cubic rather than a natural spline, because a natural spline can overshoot between two points, which in a clinical chart means drawing a value the patient never had.

Note the colour of the line. Margaret's systolic pressure is still above her usual range, and the trend renders in calm azure. Red is never a trend here. It belongs to the emergency button, the critical-value card and the delete confirmation — nowhere else on a patient surface.

Three surfaces, one system

The same tokens, the same components and the same icon family serve all three — but they are tuned to three different registers, because a frightened parent at eleven at night and a physician with thirty-two patients booked need opposite things from a screen.

Patient app

Optimised for calm. People use this frightened, in pain or exhausted, so density, motion and tone are treated as clinical variables rather than aesthetic ones.

At most seven primary elements above the fold. A denser patient screen fails review.

Sentence case, no medical abbreviations, no metaphor in safety copy.

Five household profiles, and the active one is named in the bar at all times.

Ninety-three screens across sign-in, home, booking, medicines, records and emergency.

Clinician workspace

Optimised for minutes. Dr Darlene Robertson sees thirty-two patients a day; the target is a full history in ten seconds and documentation finished before she leaves the room.

Compact density — vertical rhythm compresses, hit areas never do.

Every alert states its evidence and its recommended action, or it is noise.

An unresolved interaction blocks the signature. Allergy banners cannot be dismissed.

AI drafts are labelled, attributable, and signed by a named clinician or not at all.

Operations console

Optimised for exceptions. Gregory Sutton does not want a dashboard to browse; he wants the four things that are wrong right now pushed to the top with a clock on them.

Queues, worklists and boards — front office, pharmacy, laboratory, beds, dispatch.

A critical value is acknowledged inside sixty minutes or it escalates by itself.

No prescription is dispensed without a pharmacist verification. That is a hard invariant.

Every exception names the person, the elapsed time and the next action.

Designed for safety, visibly

These are not preferences and they are not tone. Each one is a rule in the specification that overrides aesthetic judgement in every case, each one changed the interface in a way you can point at, and each one exists because of a specific way people get hurt.

Red is never a trend

A worsening value renders in calm azure, whichever way it moved. Coral is reserved for the emergency button, the critical-value card and delete confirmations. Nobody should open this app after a bad result and be met with red.

Colour-semantics contract

The emergency affordance cannot be moved

It sits above sheets and modals at z-index 900, solid rather than glass, with no motion of any kind, two taps from anywhere. It is not personalised, not feature-flagged and not A/B tested. In this prototype 911 is shown but never dialable — a demo that rings a real emergency service is an incident.

CS-1

Unavailable times are shown, not hidden

Removing a full slot makes the day look empty and makes the patient doubt what they saw a moment ago. A greyed 10:15 marked "Taken" is the truth, and the screen offers the nearest alternative in the same breath: "That time was just booked. The closest is 11:30."

Booking contract

Doses are never abbreviated

"Take one tablet twice a day, after food" — never "500 mg BD PC". Units are always explicit, and look-alike drug names use tall-man lettering in selection lists. Abbreviation is how the wrong dose gets taken, by patients and by clinicians alike.

CS-7

The active patient is unmistakable

Their name and relationship sit in the top bar of every clinical screen, and switching profile clears whatever was on screen rather than carrying it across. Acting on the wrong family member is the most dangerous usability failure this product can have.

CS-5 · INV-02

Nothing is gamified and nothing is lost

No streaks, no decaying scores, no leaderboards, no comparison with other patients. An untaken dose says "Ready when you are", because a person who is unwell, or asleep, or in hospital has not failed at anything.

CS-10 · CS-11

It adapts to the room

There is no device picker in this prototype and no phone frame drawn around anything. The app measures the window it has been given and lays itself out accordingly, then components size themselves against their own container rather than against the window — so the same card is correct in a phone list, in a two-up grid and in a split-view column. Drag the corner of this window and watch the hand-off.

Measuring this window…

Mac and desktop You are here

A fixed 88 px Liquid Glass rail on the leading edge, carrying five destinations and Settings, and a twelve-column field capped at 1440 px. Hover states appear, focus rings matter, and split views open a third pane. This is the reference implementation — everything else is a deliberate reduction of it.

iPad and small windows You are here

The rail hands over to a floating glass tab capsule at the bottom, never more than five items and always icon plus label. Eight columns, 20 px gutters, and split views keep two panes instead of three. Touch targets grow; the visual scale does not.

iPhone You are here

One column, four grid units, and carousels only where a grid genuinely will not fit — sideways scrolling is a compromise, so it is dropped the moment there is room. Safe areas are respected on every edge, and every hit area stays at least 44 px however small the label gets.

Two more axes move independently of width. Appearance is a complete second theme rather than an inversion — in dark, elevation is expressed as lightness rather than shadow, saturation drops as luminance rises, and pure black is never used. Dynamic Type runs to 200%, at which point rows that would truncate become stacks and no text container has a fixed height to clip against. Try the appearance button in the bar, or the type-size control in Settings.

Reference

About the cast. Every person, facility, pharmacy, laboratory and phone number in this prototype comes from Appendix E of the specification — one shared cast, so a screenshot in a deck matches a fixture in a test and nobody invents "John Doe" at two in the morning. Everyone is fictional. Phone numbers use the 555 exchange and emails use example.com. Dates are absolute rather than relative, so a screenshot taken in March still makes sense in July.

About the images. There are no photographs of any patient or clinician anywhere in this product. Avatars are generated initials on a deterministic tint. The three photographs that do exist are ambient, appear only on the sign-in and onboarding surfaces, and are of nobody in particular.

About 911. It is displayed on the emergency surface, exactly as it must be, and it is never a live link. Every dialable number in the prototype points at the 555 exchange.
