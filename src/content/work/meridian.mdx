## The live prototype

Launch it and you land on Meridian's sign-in screen. The fields are pre-filled; any password and any six digits get you through. From there it behaves like a product, not a walkthrough: edit a record's status inline, drag cards between columns on the Board, press ⌘K to jump anywhere or start the guided tour.

<Margin label="Try this">
  Switch to *Acme Studio* from the org menu. You're a Guest there, and the permissions engine quietly removes what you can't do, no fake disabled states.
</Margin>

The parts that usually get faked are the parts I was most interested in building. Flip the organisation's lifecycle state in Billing and watch the paywalls and dunning banners engage. Impersonate a user in the Admin console, then open the Audit log and find the action already recorded against your name. Your changes persist across a refresh, because there's a real store underneath.

Three surfaces are reachable from the tabs in the window chrome: the **App** itself, the **Design system** it's built from, and the **Build plan** that sequenced it.

## Why it exists

Meridian started from a 20,000-word functional specification for a B2B SaaS work platform: the genre of document that defines every state and permission a system needs and, in the same breath, concedes what it can't supply. This one said so outright: the design system it described had **no signature**. Correct, accessible, and indistinguishable from every competitor in its category.

<Bleed
  quote="Correct, accessible, and indistinguishable from every competitor."
  attribution="The spec's own assessment of its design system"
/>

That gap is the whole project. A spec can enumerate requirements; it can't decide what a product should feel like, and it can't prove the requirements hold together until something is actually built. So I did both: invented an identity for it, then built the platform out module by module until it ran.

<Aside label="Why build it, not draw it">
  A static comp can't tell you whether a permissions model is coherent, or whether a paywall lands in the right place in a flow. Building it on mock data is the cheapest way to find out that the answer is no.
</Aside>

## The identity: Day-Arc

A meridian is the line the sun crosses at its highest point: the moment of peak clarity. That single image does all the work. The mark is an arc, a meridian line, and a zenith orb: a hemisphere brought to its brightest. The palette runs the whole arc of a day, warm dawn through cool dusk, where the category defaults to corporate blue.

Six colours, six named gradients, and a deliberate constraint: colour is never the sole carrier of meaning, so every semantic role ships paired with an icon or a label. Type is rounded and warm on purpose: Gabarito gives the brand its voice, Figtree keeps dense data screens legible at small sizes. Nothing about it is neutral, which was the point.

## What's under it

The prototype is a single HTML file with no framework and no server, which meant every system in it had to be built rather than imported.

<Shot
  src="/images/work/meridian/app-board.png"
  domain="meridian.app"
  align="right"
  aspect="8 / 5"
  caption="The Board view: the core work surface. Cards drag between status columns, and the change writes through the same store the List and Calendar views read."
/>

A permissions engine gates every action against the current member's role, so a Guest's interface is genuinely different rather than cosmetically dimmed. An entitlements engine enforces plan limits, and a lifecycle state machine moves an org between trial, active, past due, restricted and cancelled: with the banners, paywalls and read-only states each of those implies. An audit spine records the actions worth recording. The command palette is a real router. State is optimistic and undoable, and it persists to your browser.

<Aside label="Scope, honestly">
  Sixteen views are built and clickable. The build plan you can read in the third tab scopes considerably more than that: its 106 screens and ~355 states are the *plan's* inventory, not a claim about what's running above. The prototype covers the spine of the product, not every leaf of it.
</Aside>
